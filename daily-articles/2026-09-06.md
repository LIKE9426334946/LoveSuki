# How Migrating Birds Find Their Way

Every year, many birds travel hundreds or even thousands of kilometers between their breeding and winter homes. Some cross oceans, deserts, and mountain ranges. They cannot read maps or road signs, yet many arrive at the same places year after year. Their navigation is not based on one special ability. Instead, birds combine several kinds of information from the sky, the Earth, and their own experience.

The sun is one useful guide. Birds can observe its position and use it like a compass. Because the sun moves across the sky during the day, they must also have a sense of time. Their internal body clock helps them adjust the direction they follow as the hours pass. On a cloudy day, however, the sun may not be visible, so birds need other methods.

Many species migrate at night and use the stars. Young birds appear to learn the pattern of the night sky rather than following one bright star. The rotation of the stars around a central point helps them identify north. Experiments in special indoor rooms have shown that changing the visible star pattern can change the direction in which migrating birds try to fly.

Birds may also sense the Earth’s magnetic field. This invisible field surrounds the planet and points roughly from north to south. Scientists are still studying exactly how birds detect it. One idea is that special light-sensitive proteins in their eyes help them “see” magnetic information as a faint pattern. Another possibility is that tiny magnetic particles in their bodies provide directional signals.

Landmarks become especially useful near familiar areas. Coastlines, rivers, mountains, and even roads can help experienced birds recognize a route. Smell may play a role for some species as well. Older birds often navigate more accurately than young ones because they have already learned which paths are safe and where food and resting places can be found.

Migration is still risky. Strong wind can push a bird away from its path, and artificial lights may confuse birds flying at night. Bad weather, lack of food, and the loss of natural resting areas create additional challenges. To prepare for the journey, many birds eat more than usual and store energy as fat. Some also travel in groups, which may help them find good routes and reduce danger.

No single signal is reliable in every situation. The sun can disappear behind clouds, stars can be hidden, and landscapes can change. By using several navigation systems together, birds can compare the information and correct mistakes. Their journeys show how animals solve a complex problem through a mixture of natural senses, learning, and constant adjustment.

## Important Vocabulary

1. **migrate** — to travel regularly from one region to another; 迁徙
2. **breeding** — relating to producing and raising young animals; 繁殖的
3. **navigation** — the process of finding and following a route; 导航，辨别方向
4. **compass** — a tool or natural guide used to find direction; 指南针
5. **internal** — existing or happening inside something; 内部的
6. **rotation** — the movement of turning around a central point; 旋转
7. **magnetic** — related to the force of magnetism; 磁性的
8. **landmark** — a recognizable feature that helps someone identify a place; 地标
9. **accurately** — in a correct and exact way; 准确地
10. **reliable** — able to be trusted or depended on; 可靠的

## Useful Grammar and Sentence Patterns

1. **“rather than + verb-ing”**  
   Use this pattern to show that one action happens instead of another.  
   Example: “Young birds appear to learn the pattern of the night sky rather than following one bright star.”

2. **“One idea is that …”**  
   Use this pattern to introduce one possible explanation.  
   Example: “One idea is that special light-sensitive proteins in their eyes help them see magnetic information.”

3. **“By + verb-ing, + result”**  
   Use this pattern to explain how a result is achieved.  
   Example: “By using several navigation systems together, birds can compare the information and correct mistakes.”
