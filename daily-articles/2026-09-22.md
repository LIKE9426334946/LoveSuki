# How an Airplane Cabin Lets You Breathe

Passenger airplanes often fly about ten or eleven kilometers above the ground. At that height, the outside air is cold and extremely thin. A person could not breathe normally for long without extra oxygen. Yet inside the cabin, passengers can talk, eat, and sleep comfortably. This is possible because the aircraft controls the air pressure around them.

Air pressure is created by countless gas molecules moving and hitting surfaces. Near sea level, many air molecules are packed into a given space. As altitude increases, the air becomes less dense, so fewer oxygen molecules enter the lungs with each breath. The percentage of oxygen in the atmosphere remains close to 21 percent, but its pressure becomes too low for the body to use it effectively.

An airplane cabin is therefore pressurized. During flight, compressed air is supplied to the cabin. On many jet aircraft, this air comes from a compression stage in the engines. Some newer designs use electric compressors instead. Before the air reaches passengers, systems cool it, control its temperature, and mix it with filtered cabin air.

The cabin is not completely sealed. Fresh air enters continuously, and some air is allowed to leave through an outflow valve. By carefully controlling how much air escapes, the aircraft maintains a higher pressure inside than outside. This pressure is usually similar to what people experience on a mountain around 1,800 to 2,400 meters high, although the exact level depends on the aircraft and flight conditions.

Why not keep the cabin at normal sea-level pressure? A larger difference between inside and outside pressure would place more force on the aircraft's body. Every flight also adds another cycle of pressure and release. Engineers must balance passenger comfort, structural strength, weight, and energy use. The rounded shape of the fuselage helps spread the force, while small windows with curved corners reduce areas where stress could become concentrated.

Changes in cabin pressure can make your ears feel blocked, especially during descent. Air exists on both sides of the eardrum, but the pressures may not change at the same speed. Swallowing, yawning, or chewing can open a narrow passage called the Eustachian tube and help equalize the pressure. Blowing very gently while holding the nose closed can also help, but forceful blowing may cause pain or injury.

If cabin pressure falls unexpectedly, oxygen masks drop from above the seats. They provide a temporary source of oxygen while pilots quickly move the airplane to a lower altitude. Passengers are told to put on their own masks first because clear thinking can become difficult before a person fully notices the lack of oxygen.

Cabin pressurization turns a dangerous high-altitude environment into a space where the human body can function. It is not simply a matter of pumping in air. Valves, compressors, sensors, filters, and the aircraft structure must work together throughout the flight.

## Important Vocabulary

1. **altitude** — height above sea level or the ground; 海拔，高度
2. **dense** — containing a large amount of material in a small space; 密集的，密度大的
3. **pressurized** — kept at a controlled pressure higher than the surrounding environment; 加压的，增压的
4. **compressed** — pressed into a smaller space; 被压缩的
5. **outflow valve** — a device that controls how much air leaves the cabin; 排气阀
6. **structural** — related to the way something is built and supported; 结构上的
7. **fuselage** — the main body of an aircraft; 机身
8. **eardrum** — the thin part inside the ear that vibrates when sound reaches it; 鼓膜
9. **equalize** — to make two amounts or pressures equal; 使相等，使平衡
10. **unexpectedly** — in a way that was not planned or predicted; 出乎意料地

## Useful Grammar and Sentence Patterns

1. **“As + noun + verb, + result”**  
   Use this pattern to show that one change happens together with another.  
   Example: “As altitude increases, the air becomes less dense.”

2. **“By + verb-ing, + subject + verb”**  
   Use this pattern to explain the method used to achieve a result.  
   Example: “By carefully controlling how much air escapes, the aircraft maintains a higher pressure inside.”

3. **“not simply a matter of + verb-ing”**  
   Use this pattern to show that a process is more complex than it first appears.  
   Example: “It is not simply a matter of pumping in air.”
