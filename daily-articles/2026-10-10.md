# Why Honey Can Last for So Long

Most fresh foods change quickly. Fruit becomes soft, bread grows mold, and cooked food may develop unpleasant smells. Honey is different. When it is stored in a clean, closed container, it can remain usable for an extremely long time. Its stability is not caused by one magical ingredient. It comes from several chemical and biological conditions working together.

Honey begins as flower nectar, a watery liquid that contains sugars. A bee collects the nectar and carries it in a special part of its digestive system called the honey stomach. Enzymes from the bee begin changing some of the complex sugars into simpler ones. Back at the hive, bees pass the nectar between one another and place it inside wax cells.

Fresh nectar contains too much water to last like finished honey. Bees repeatedly move air across the open cells with their wings. Water slowly evaporates, and the liquid becomes thicker. When the moisture level is low enough, the bees cover the cell with wax. This process turns a watery plant liquid into a highly concentrated sugar solution.

The low amount of available water is one of honey's strongest protections. Microorganisms such as bacteria and mold need water for their chemical reactions and growth. Honey does contain some water, but most of it is surrounded by sugar molecules and is difficult for microorganisms to use.

Honey's high sugar concentration creates another problem for microbial cells. Through a process called osmosis, water tends to move across a cell membrane toward the area with more dissolved material. If a microorganism enters honey, water may be pulled out of its cells. This makes normal growth very difficult.

Acidity adds further protection. Honey is usually acidic because it contains several natural acids, including gluconic acid. Many harmful microorganisms grow best in conditions that are closer to neutral. Honey's lower pH creates an environment in which they cannot easily multiply.

Honey also contains small amounts of substances that limit microbial growth. An enzyme added by bees can help produce hydrogen peroxide, especially when honey is mixed with some water. Different honeys contain different plant chemicals as well. These effects support preservation, although the low available water and concentrated sugar remain especially important.

This does not mean that honey is impossible to damage. Because it attracts moisture from the air, an open jar can slowly absorb water. If enough water enters, certain yeasts may begin to grow and cause fermentation. Food, saliva, or dirty utensils can also introduce moisture and other material. Honey should therefore be kept tightly closed, and it is best to use a clean, dry spoon.

Honey often becomes cloudy, thick, or grainy during storage. This is usually crystallization rather than spoilage. Honey contains large amounts of glucose and fructose. Glucose is less soluble and can gradually form solid crystals, while much of the fructose stays liquid. The speed of crystallization depends on temperature, water content, and the honey's natural composition.

Crystallized honey can usually be softened by placing the closed container in warm water and heating it gently. High heat is unnecessary and can change its flavor and aroma. Crystallization may alter the texture, but it does not remove honey's basic usefulness.

Honey lasts because bees create an environment that is dry, sugary, acidic, and difficult for microorganisms. A jar of honey is therefore both a food and an impressive example of natural preservation.

## Important Vocabulary

1. **nectar** — a sweet liquid produced by flowers; 花蜜
2. **enzyme** — a protein that helps a chemical reaction happen more quickly; 酶
3. **evaporate** — to change from a liquid into a gas; 蒸发
4. **concentrated** — containing a large amount of a substance in a small volume; 浓缩的
5. **microorganism** — a living thing too small to be seen clearly without a microscope; 微生物
6. **osmosis** — the movement of water through a membrane toward a more concentrated solution; 渗透作用
7. **acidic** — having the chemical qualities of an acid and a pH below seven; 酸性的
8. **fermentation** — a process in which microorganisms change sugars into other substances; 发酵
9. **crystallization** — the process of forming solid crystals; 结晶
10. **soluble** — able to dissolve in a liquid; 可溶的

## Useful Grammar and Sentence Patterns

1. **“When + condition, + result”**  
   Use this pattern to explain what happens under a particular condition.  
   Example: “When the moisture level is low enough, the bees cover the cell with wax.”

2. **“If + present condition, + may + verb”**  
   Use this pattern to describe a possible result of a real condition.  
   Example: “If enough water enters, certain yeasts may begin to grow.”

3. **“rather than + noun”**  
   Use this pattern to correct a likely misunderstanding.  
   Example: “This is usually crystallization rather than spoilage.”
