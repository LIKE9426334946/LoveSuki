# How an Induction Cooktop Heats a Pan

An induction cooktop can boil water quickly even though there is no flame and no red-hot heating element. The smooth glass surface may look almost unchanged while the pan becomes very hot. The reason is that the cooktop does not mainly heat the glass first. Instead, it uses a changing magnetic field to create heat inside the cookware itself.

Beneath the glass is a coil made of copper wire. When the cooktop is switched on, an alternating electric current moves through this coil. The current changes direction thousands of times each second. This rapid change produces a magnetic field that also changes direction again and again.

When a suitable pan is placed above the coil, the changing magnetic field passes through its metal base. This causes small electric currents to flow around inside the metal. These are often called eddy currents. The metal resists their movement, and that resistance changes electrical energy into heat. Magnetic materials can also lose a little energy as their internal magnetic direction changes. Both effects warm the pan, which then heats the food.

Not every pan works on an induction cooktop. Iron, cast iron, and many kinds of stainless steel are compatible because a magnet can strongly affect them. Aluminum, copper, glass, and ceramic cookware usually will not work by themselves. However, manufacturers can add a magnetic layer to the base, making these pans suitable for induction cooking. A simple test is to place a refrigerator magnet against the bottom of a pan. If it sticks firmly, the pan will probably work.

Induction cooking is often efficient because energy is produced directly in the cookware. Less heat is wasted in the air around the pan, and changes in the power setting affect cooking quickly. The cooktop can also detect whether suitable cookware is present. If the pan is removed, many models stop sending energy to that area.

Still, the glass surface is not completely cool or risk-free. A hot pan transfers heat back into the glass, so the area may remain hot after cooking. Metal objects such as spoons should not be left on an active cooking zone. People with certain implanted medical devices should follow the advice of the device manufacturer and their doctor because strong electromagnetic fields may affect some equipment.

An induction cooktop may produce soft buzzing or clicking sounds. The magnetic field can make parts of the pan vibrate slightly, especially at high power. A cooling fan may also continue running after cooking to protect the electronic parts. These sounds are usually normal.

For good results, use a pan with a flat, clean base and place it in the center of the marked zone. Choose a pan that is close to the recommended size, and lift it instead of sliding it to protect the glass. Induction cooking may seem mysterious, but its basic path is clear: electricity creates a changing magnetic field, the field creates currents in the pan, and the pan's resistance creates heat.

## Important Vocabulary

1. **induction** — the production of an electric effect through a changing magnetic field; 电磁感应
2. **cooktop** — the flat upper surface of a cooker where food is heated; 炉灶台，灶面
3. **coil** — wire wound into a series of circles; 线圈
4. **alternating current** — electric current that repeatedly changes direction; 交流电
5. **magnetic field** — the area around a magnet or electric current where magnetic force acts; 磁场
6. **cookware** — pots, pans, and other containers used for cooking; 炊具
7. **resistance** — the opposition to the flow of electric current; 电阻
8. **compatible** — able to work correctly with something else; 兼容的，适用的
9. **efficient** — working well without wasting much energy, time, or material; 高效的
10. **vibrate** — to move quickly backward and forward; 振动

## Useful Grammar and Sentence Patterns

1. **“The reason is that + clause”**  
   Use this pattern to introduce an explanation for a surprising fact.  
   Example: “The reason is that the cooktop does not mainly heat the glass first.”

2. **“If + condition, + result”**  
   Use this pattern to explain what happens when a condition is met.  
   Example: “If it sticks firmly, the pan will probably work.”

3. **“instead of + verb-ing”**  
   Use this pattern to recommend one action in place of another.  
   Example: “Lift it instead of sliding it to protect the glass.”
