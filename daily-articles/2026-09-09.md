# How GPS Knows Where You Are

When you open a map on your phone, a small blue dot often appears within seconds. It may seem as if your phone sends a question to a satellite and receives its location as an answer. In reality, GPS works differently. Your phone mainly listens to carefully timed radio signals and calculates its own position from them.

GPS stands for Global Positioning System. It uses a group of satellites that travel around the Earth along known paths. Each satellite carries extremely accurate clocks and repeatedly broadcasts two important pieces of information: where the satellite is and when the signal was sent. A GPS receiver in your phone compares the sending time with the arrival time.

Radio signals travel at approximately the speed of light. By measuring how long a signal took to arrive, the receiver can estimate its distance from the satellite. Even a tiny timing error matters because light travels about 300,000 kilometers in one second. A difference of one millionth of a second represents roughly 300 meters.

Knowing the distance from one satellite is not enough. Imagine that a satellite is at the center of a huge invisible sphere. Your phone could be anywhere on the sphere’s surface. A second satellite creates another sphere, and the possible locations become fewer. A third distance narrows the choices further. This method is called trilateration because it uses distances rather than angles.

In practice, a receiver usually needs signals from at least four satellites. The fourth signal helps correct the phone’s clock, which is far less accurate than the clocks in the satellites. With enough good signals, the receiver can calculate latitude, longitude, altitude, and time. Modern phones may also use additional satellite navigation systems, mobile towers, and nearby Wi-Fi networks to improve speed or accuracy.

GPS performs best when the receiver has a clear view of the sky. Buildings, mountains, and trees can block or reflect radio signals. Reflected signals travel a longer path and may make the receiver calculate an incorrect distance. Indoors, the signals are often too weak to pass through walls effectively. This is why the blue dot may move around or become less accurate inside a large building.

The GPS receiver calculates coordinates, but it does not create the map itself. A mapping application places those coordinates onto stored road and place data. It may then compare your movement with nearby roads to guess which route you are following.

GPS is a remarkable system because several devices must work together with great precision. Satellites thousands of kilometers away broadcast their time and position, while a tiny receiver uses differences measured in fractions of a second to locate you on Earth.

## Important Vocabulary

1. **satellite** — an object that moves around a planet or another body in space; 卫星
2. **broadcast** — to send information by radio, television, or another signal; 广播，发送
3. **receiver** — a device that accepts incoming signals; 接收器
4. **approximately** — close to a particular number but not exact; 大约，近似地
5. **estimate** — to calculate or judge an amount roughly; 估算
6. **sphere** — a perfectly round three-dimensional shape; 球体
7. **trilateration** — finding a position by measuring distances from known points; 三边测量定位
8. **altitude** — height above sea level or the ground; 海拔，高度
9. **reflect** — to send light, sound, or another wave back from a surface; 反射
10. **coordinate** — a number used to describe an exact position; 坐标

## Useful Grammar and Sentence Patterns

1. **“By + verb-ing, + subject + can …”**  
   Use this pattern to explain the method used to achieve something.  
   Example: “By measuring how long a signal took to arrive, the receiver can estimate its distance from the satellite.”

2. **“far less + adjective + than …”**  
   Use this pattern to make a strong comparison between two things.  
   Example: “The phone’s clock is far less accurate than the clocks in the satellites.”

3. **“This is why + result”**  
   Use this pattern to connect an explanation with its visible result.  
   Example: “This is why the blue dot may move around or become less accurate inside a large building.”
