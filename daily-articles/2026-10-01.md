# Why Clouds Do Not Fall from the Sky

A large cloud may contain an enormous amount of water, yet it can remain high above the ground for hours. From a distance, it may look like a light object floating in the air. In reality, a cloud is not one solid body. It is a wide collection of extremely small water droplets, ice crystals, or both.

Clouds begin when moist air rises and cools. Cooler air cannot keep as much water in the form of invisible vapor. Some of the vapor therefore condenses onto tiny particles such as dust, salt, or smoke. Each particle becomes the center of a microscopic droplet. When billions of these droplets gather in one region, they scatter light and form a cloud that we can see.

Gravity does pull every droplet downward. However, an ordinary cloud droplet is so small that it falls very slowly. Air resistance has a strong effect on such a tiny object. After falling a short distance, the droplet reaches a low terminal speed, meaning that it no longer accelerates. Even weak upward air movement can match or exceed this downward speed.

The air inside and around a cloud is rarely still. Warm air may continue rising from below, while winds mix air in many directions. These air currents keep droplets moving and can carry them upward. The droplets are falling relative to the nearby air, but the air itself may be rising. As a result, their overall position may change very little.

Clouds are also constantly changing. At one edge, droplets may evaporate and become invisible water vapor again. Elsewhere, new droplets form as rising air cools. A cloud can therefore appear to stay in one place even though its individual droplets are forming, moving, and disappearing all the time. It is similar to a waterfall that keeps its general shape while different water passes through it.

Eventually, some droplets become large enough to fall faster. In warm clouds, they may collide and join together. In cold parts of a cloud, ice crystals can grow as water vapor collects on them. They may also combine with other crystals or collect liquid droplets that freeze. As these particles become heavier, ordinary air currents can no longer keep them high in the cloud.

This is when precipitation begins. Depending on the temperatures inside the cloud and below it, falling particles may reach the ground as rain, snow, sleet, or hail. Some drops never arrive. If the air underneath is dry, they may evaporate while falling, creating visible streaks below a cloud that fade before reaching the surface.

A dark cloud often looks heavier than a white one, but color does not directly measure weight. Thick clouds block and scatter more sunlight, so their lower surfaces appear darker. Such clouds may contain larger droplets and more water, which can be a sign that rain is developing, but darkness itself is mainly an effect of light.

Clouds remain in the sky because their tiny particles fall slowly and move within active air. They finally produce rain or snow only after those particles grow large enough to overcome the upward movement and resistance of the atmosphere.

## Important Vocabulary

1. **droplet** — a very small drop of liquid; 小液滴
2. **crystal** — a solid whose particles are arranged in a regular pattern; 晶体
3. **vapor** — a substance in its gas form, especially one usually found as a liquid; 蒸气
4. **condense** — to change from a gas into a liquid; 凝结
5. **microscopic** — extremely small and difficult or impossible to see without a microscope; 微小的，显微镜下可见的
6. **gravity** — the force that pulls objects toward the Earth; 重力
7. **resistance** — a force that opposes or slows movement; 阻力
8. **current** — a continuous movement of air or water in one direction; 气流，水流
9. **evaporate** — to change from a liquid into a gas; 蒸发
10. **precipitation** — water that falls from the atmosphere as rain, snow, sleet, or hail; 降水

## Useful Grammar and Sentence Patterns

1. **“so + adjective + that + clause”**  
   Use this pattern to show that a quality causes a particular result.  
   Example: “An ordinary cloud droplet is so small that it falls very slowly.”

2. **“even though + clause”**  
   Use this pattern to introduce a fact that contrasts with the main statement.  
   Example: “A cloud can appear to stay in one place even though its droplets are constantly changing.”

3. **“Depending on + noun, + clause”**  
   Use this pattern when the result changes according to a condition.  
   Example: “Depending on the temperatures, particles may reach the ground as rain, snow, sleet, or hail.”
