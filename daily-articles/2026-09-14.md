# Why Your Fingers Wrinkle in Water

After a long bath or swim, the skin on your fingers and toes becomes covered with lines and folds. Many people think this happens simply because the skin absorbs water and grows larger. Water absorption does play a small part, but it does not fully explain the special pattern. The deeper cause involves your nervous system and blood vessels.

The outer layer of skin contains dead cells filled with a protein called keratin. These cells can absorb some water, especially on the hands and feet, where the outer layer is thick. As they take in water, they expand slightly. However, this swelling alone cannot produce all the deep wrinkles that appear after several minutes underwater.

The main change happens below the surface. Water enters tiny channels in the skin and changes the balance of salt there. This sends a message through the nerves. In response, the autonomic nervous system—the part that controls many actions without conscious thought—causes small blood vessels in the fingers and toes to contract. Less blood flows through these vessels, so the tissue under the skin takes up less space. The skin above it then forms folds.

Scientists learned about this process partly by studying people with damaged nerves. If certain nerves leading to a finger are injured, that finger may not wrinkle normally in water. Doctors have even used this response as one way to examine whether particular nerves are working.

Why would the body develop such a reaction? One possible explanation is that wrinkles improve our grip in wet conditions. The channels between the folds may move water away from the point where the skin touches an object, rather like the grooves on a car tire. In some experiments, people with wrinkled fingers handled wet objects faster than people with smooth fingers. However, the advantage is not found in every study, so scientists are still discussing its exact purpose.

The reaction is strongest on fingers and toes, the body parts most often used to hold objects or walk on wet ground. It also takes several minutes to begin. This delay may prevent unnecessary wrinkling during brief contact with water. Constant wrinkles could reduce sensitivity or make the skin easier to damage.

Once you leave the water, the salt balance returns to normal, the blood vessels widen again, and the wrinkles gradually disappear. This temporary change is usually completely normal. It is a small example of how the body adjusts to its environment without requiring a conscious decision.

The next time your hands wrinkle in water, look closely at the pattern. What seems like simple wet skin is actually evidence of communication among the skin, nerves, blood vessels, and brain.

## Important Vocabulary

1. **wrinkle** — a small line or fold in skin or material; 皱纹，褶皱
2. **absorb** — to take in a liquid or another substance; 吸收
3. **outer** — located on or near the outside of something; 外部的，外层的
4. **vessel** — a tube that carries blood through the body; 血管
5. **autonomic** — controlling body processes without conscious effort; 自主的
6. **contract** — to become smaller, shorter, or tighter; 收缩
7. **tissue** — a group of similar cells that perform a particular function; 组织
8. **grip** — the ability or action of holding something firmly; 抓握，握力
9. **groove** — a long, narrow cut or channel in a surface; 凹槽，沟纹
10. **sensitivity** — the ability to notice or react to small changes; 敏感度，感知能力

## Useful Grammar and Sentence Patterns

1. **“Many people think …, but …”**  
   Use this pattern to introduce a common idea and then correct or limit it.  
   Example: “Many people think this happens simply because the skin absorbs water, but it does not fully explain the pattern.”

2. **“In response, + subject + verb”**  
   Use this pattern to describe an action caused by an earlier event.  
   Example: “In response, the autonomic nervous system causes small blood vessels to contract.”

3. **“rather like + noun”**  
   Use this pattern to make a comparison that helps explain how something works.  
   Example: “The channels may move water away, rather like the grooves on a car tire.”
