# Why Chicken Eggs Have Different Shell Colors

Chicken eggs can be white, cream, light brown, dark brown, blue, green, or even covered with spots. In a shop, people sometimes assume that one color is healthier or more natural than another. In fact, shell color is mainly controlled by the hen's genes and by pigments added while the egg is being formed.

An eggshell is made mostly of calcium carbonate, the same mineral found in chalk and many seashells. The shell begins forming inside a part of the hen's reproductive system often called the shell gland. Over many hours, calcium and other materials are built into a hard protective layer. Without added pigment, this layer is basically white.

Different breeds of chicken produce different pigments. Brown eggs receive a reddish-brown pigment called protoporphyrin. Much of it is placed on the outer part of the shell near the end of formation. This is why the inside of many brown shells looks much lighter than the outside. Some hens also create darker spots when pigment is deposited unevenly.

Blue eggs get their color from a pigment called biliverdin. It is added earlier and can spread through more of the shell, so a blue shell may look blue on both sides. Green or olive eggs are produced when brown pigment is added over a blue shell. The final shade depends on the amount and combination of pigments.

Genetics is the main reason one hen regularly lays a particular color. However, the exact shade can change. A hen's age, health, stress level, and laying pattern may affect how much pigment is available. Brown eggs from the same hen may become lighter over a laying season. The color of a chicken's feathers or earlobes can sometimes offer a clue, but it is not a reliable rule for every breed.

Shell color does not tell you how nutritious the egg is. Eggs of different colors have generally similar protein, fat, vitamins, and minerals when the hens receive similar food and care. The color of the yolk is a separate matter. Yellow and orange pigments in a hen's diet can make the yolk darker, but they do not determine whether the shell is white or brown.

Hens do need calcium to build shells, but they do not turn swallowed stones directly into eggshells. Small stones or grit may stay in a muscular organ called the gizzard, where they help crush food. Calcium is absorbed from feed or calcium-rich materials into the bloodstream and then carried to the shell gland. Vitamin D and the hen's health also affect shell strength.

When choosing eggs, freshness, storage, cleanliness, and shell condition matter more than color. A brown egg is not automatically better than a white one, and a blue egg is not a different kind of food. The varied colors are simply visible signs of chicken genetics and a carefully timed process inside the hen.

## Important Vocabulary

1. **shell** — the hard outer covering of an egg; 蛋壳，外壳
2. **pigment** — a substance that gives color to living tissue or material; 色素
3. **gene** — a unit of inherited information passed from parents to offspring; 基因
4. **calcium carbonate** — a mineral that forms most of an eggshell; 碳酸钙
5. **reproductive** — related to producing young or forming eggs; 生殖的，繁殖的
6. **deposit** — to place or leave a layer of material somewhere; 沉积，附着
7. **unevenly** — not equally or smoothly across an area; 不均匀地
8. **shade** — a particular form or degree of a color; 色调，深浅
9. **yolk** — the yellow or orange center of an egg; 蛋黄
10. **gizzard** — a strong muscular organ that helps a bird grind food; 砂囊，肌胃

## Useful Grammar and Sentence Patterns

1. **“This is why + clause”**  
   Use this pattern to connect a cause with its result.  
   Example: “This is why the inside of many brown shells looks much lighter than the outside.”

2. **“depends on + noun”**  
   Use this pattern when a result is controlled or influenced by particular factors.  
   Example: “The final shade depends on the amount and combination of pigments.”

3. **“not automatically + comparative adjective + than”**  
   Use this pattern to correct a comparison that people may assume is true.  
   Example: “A brown egg is not automatically better than a white one.”
