# Why You See Colors After Looking Away

Stare at a bright red shape for twenty seconds, then look at a white wall. You may briefly see a similar shape in green or blue-green. Nothing has been placed on the wall, yet the colored image seems real. This effect is called a negative afterimage, and it reveals how the eyes and brain adjust to visual information.

Light enters the eye and reaches the retina, a thin layer of light-sensitive tissue at the back of the eye. The retina contains cells called rods and cones. Rods are especially useful in dim light, while cones help us see fine detail and color. Most people have three main types of cones, which respond most strongly to different ranges of light that we commonly describe as red, green, and blue.

These cone types do not work like three separate paint boxes. The brain compares their activity to understand color. For example, light from a yellow object strongly activates the cones that respond to longer and medium wavelengths. The brain interprets the pattern of signals as yellow.

When you stare at one color, the cone cells responding strongly to it gradually become less sensitive. This process is called adaptation. The cells do not stop working completely, but their response becomes weaker while the unchanging color remains in view. Adaptation helps the visual system remain useful in many lighting conditions instead of reacting strongly to the same signal forever.

The change becomes noticeable when you suddenly look at a neutral white surface. White light normally activates all three cone types in a balanced way. However, the cones that were highly active a moment earlier are now temporarily less responsive. The other cone signals become relatively stronger, so the balance has changed. Your brain interprets this new pattern as the opposite, or complementary, color.

Color vision also involves opponent channels in the nervous system. Some nerve pathways compare red with green, while others compare blue with yellow. There is also a channel that compares light with dark. Activity on one side of a channel can reduce the response on the other side. When the original color disappears, the system may briefly shift toward its opponent. This strengthens the afterimage effect.

An afterimage often keeps the shape of the object because different parts of the retina adapted to different parts of the scene. If you move your eyes while staring, the image may become weaker because the same retinal cells are no longer receiving a perfectly steady pattern. Blinking or looking around usually makes the afterimage disappear within seconds.

Positive afterimages can occur too. They keep colors similar to the original image and are often caused by a very brief, bright view. A camera flash may leave a light shape in your vision because activity in the visual system continues for a short time after the light is gone.

You can explore negative afterimages safely with a colored square on a screen or a piece of paper under normal lighting. Look near its center for fifteen to twenty seconds, then shift your eyes to a blank white area. Avoid staring at the Sun, powerful lamps, lasers, or other dangerously bright sources, because they can injure the retina.

Afterimages show that vision is not a passive recording. The brain continually compares signals, adjusts sensitivity, and builds your experience of color from changing patterns of activity.

## Important Vocabulary

1. **afterimage** — an image that remains briefly after the original object is no longer being viewed; 后像，残像
2. **retina** — the light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye; 视网膜
3. **cone** — a retinal cell that helps detect color and fine detail; 视锥细胞
4. **rod** — a retinal cell that is especially sensitive in dim light; 视杆细胞
5. **wavelength** — the distance between repeating points in a wave, related to the color of light; 波长
6. **adaptation** — adjustment to a condition or repeated stimulus; 适应
7. **responsive** — reacting quickly or strongly to something; 有反应的，敏感的
8. **complementary** — forming an opposite or balancing pair, especially in color; 互补的
9. **opponent** — acting against or in contrast with something else; 对立的
10. **sensitivity** — the ability to notice or react to small changes; 敏感度

## Useful Grammar and Sentence Patterns

1. **“while + clause”**  
   Use this pattern to contrast two facts or actions.  
   Example: “Rods are especially useful in dim light, while cones help us see fine detail and color.”

2. **“instead of + verb-ing”**  
   Use this pattern to show that one action or result replaces another.  
   Example: “Adaptation helps the visual system remain useful instead of reacting strongly to the same signal forever.”

3. **“because + clause”**  
   Use this pattern to give a clear reason for a result.  
   Example: “The image may become weaker because the same retinal cells are no longer receiving a steady pattern.”
