# Why Static Electricity Makes Your Hair Stand Up

Rub a balloon on your hair, and something strange may happen. Your hair is attracted to the balloon, and individual hairs begin to rise and spread apart. You may also hear tiny cracks when removing a sweater or feel a quick shock after walking across a carpet. These effects are caused by static electricity: an imbalance of electric charge that stays in one place for a short time.

Everything around us is made of atoms. Each atom contains positively charged protons and negatively charged electrons. Protons are held tightly inside the center of an atom, while some electrons can move between materials. Normally, an object has equal amounts of positive and negative charge, so its overall charge is neutral.

When two different materials touch and separate, electrons may move from one surface to the other. Rubbing increases the amount of contact, so more electrons can be transferred. If a balloon gains electrons from your hair, the balloon becomes negatively charged. Your hair loses electrons and becomes positively charged.

Opposite charges attract each other, which explains why your hair moves toward the balloon. At the same time, all the hairs have a similar positive charge. Similar charges repel, so the hairs push away from one another. Because hair is light and flexible, this electrical force can lift it against gravity and create the familiar standing-up effect.

Static electricity is often more noticeable in dry weather. Moist air leaves a thin layer of water on many surfaces. This water allows charge to move away gradually, preventing a large imbalance from building up. Dry air is a poorer path for charge, so extra electrons can remain on an object or person for longer. That is why shocks from doors or metal objects are common during winter in heated rooms.

A shock occurs when the stored charge suddenly finds a path between objects. After walking on a carpet, your body may carry extra electrons. When your finger approaches a metal door handle, the electric field can become strong enough for electrons to jump through the air. The movement happens quickly and may produce a small spark, sound, and painful feeling.

Most everyday static shocks are harmless because they contain very little energy. However, static discharge can damage sensitive electronic parts or start a fire near fuel vapors. People who work with computer components often wear a grounded wrist strap, which gives charge a safe path into the ground. Touching a grounded metal surface before handling electronics can also reduce risk.

Lightning is a much larger example of electric charge building up and suddenly moving, although the processes inside storm clouds are more complex. The tiny spark from your finger and a flash of lightning differ greatly in size, but both show the same basic idea: separated charges naturally seek a path back toward balance.

## Important Vocabulary

1. **static** — staying in one place instead of moving continuously; 静止的，静电的
2. **imbalance** — a state in which two related amounts are not equal; 不平衡
3. **charge** — an electrical property of matter that can be positive or negative; 电荷
4. **neutral** — having no overall positive or negative electric charge; 电中性的
5. **transfer** — to move something from one place or object to another; 转移，传递
6. **attract** — to pull something closer through a force; 吸引
7. **repel** — to push something away through a force; 排斥
8. **flexible** — able to bend easily without breaking; 柔韧的，灵活的
9. **discharge** — the sudden release or movement of stored electricity; 放电
10. **grounded** — connected to the earth so that electric charge can flow away safely; 接地的

## Useful Grammar and Sentence Patterns

1. **“When + event, + result”**  
   Use this pattern to explain what happens after or during a particular event.  
   Example: “When two different materials touch and separate, electrons may move from one surface to the other.”

2. **“which explains why …”**  
   Use this pattern to connect a fact with the result it helps us understand.  
   Example: “Opposite charges attract each other, which explains why your hair moves toward the balloon.”

3. **“strong enough for + noun + to + verb”**  
   Use this pattern when something reaches the level needed to cause an action.  
   Example: “The electric field can become strong enough for electrons to jump through the air.”
