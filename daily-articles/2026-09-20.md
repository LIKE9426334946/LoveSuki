# How Geckos Walk on Walls

A gecko can run across a wall and even hang upside down from a ceiling. It does this without glue, claws, or sticky liquid. Its feet may look ordinary from a distance, but under a microscope they reveal a remarkable system of tiny structures. Together, these structures allow the animal to use a weak molecular force millions of times at once.

The bottom of each gecko toe has wide ridges. These ridges are covered with very small hairs called setae. A single gecko may have millions of them. Each hair divides near its end into hundreds of even smaller branches with flat tips. These tips are so tiny that they can come extremely close to the molecules of a surface.

When two molecules are close together, their electrical charges can briefly affect each other. This creates a weak attraction known as a van der Waals force. The force between one hair tip and a wall is extremely small. However, a gecko places an enormous number of tips against the surface at the same time. The combined attraction is strong enough to support its body.

Gecko feet are therefore not sticky in the way that tape is sticky. They do not leave material behind, and dirt does not permanently destroy their ability to attach. The system depends mainly on close contact. A smooth surface gives many hair tips a chance to touch, while a very rough surface may leave fewer contact points.

If the attraction is strong, how does a gecko lift its foot to take the next step? The answer involves angle and movement. The animal presses its toes down and pulls them slightly in one direction to create strong contact. To release a foot, it changes the angle of the hairs and peels its toes away from the surface. This process requires little effort and can happen very quickly. As a result, a gecko can run instead of slowly pulling each foot free.

Its toes also help it manage dust. When a gecko walks, some dirt particles are more strongly attracted to the ground than to the tiny hairs. Repeated steps can therefore remove part of the dirt naturally. This self-cleaning ability keeps the feet useful in real environments, although water, oil, and certain surfaces can still reduce their performance.

Scientists and engineers study gecko feet because the same idea could improve human technology. Researchers have developed dry adhesives, climbing robots, and gripping tools inspired by their structure. Such materials could hold objects without wet glue and release them without leaving marks. Designing them is difficult because artificial hairs must be extremely small, flexible, strong, and arranged correctly.

The gecko shows how nature can build a powerful system from a very weak force. One molecular attraction is almost meaningless, but millions working together can hold an animal to a ceiling—and let it run away in an instant.

## Important Vocabulary

1. **gecko** — a small lizard that often has special feet for climbing; 壁虎
2. **microscope** — an instrument that makes very small objects appear larger; 显微镜
3. **ridge** — a long, narrow raised part of a surface; 脊状突起，隆起
4. **setae** — tiny hair-like structures on an animal or plant; 刚毛，微小毛状结构
5. **molecule** — the smallest unit of a substance that keeps its chemical properties; 分子
6. **attraction** — a force that pulls things toward each other; 吸引力
7. **combined** — joined together to produce a single result; 结合的，共同的
8. **attach** — to join or fasten one thing to another; 附着，连接
9. **peel** — to remove something by pulling it away from a surface; 剥离，揭开
10. **adhesive** — a substance or material used to make things stick together; 黏合剂，黏附材料

## Useful Grammar and Sentence Patterns

1. **“so + adjective + that …”**  
   Use this pattern to show that a quality causes a particular result.  
   Example: “These tips are so tiny that they can come extremely close to the molecules of a surface.”

2. **“strong enough to + verb”**  
   Use this pattern to say that something has the necessary strength or degree for an action.  
   Example: “The combined attraction is strong enough to support its body.”

3. **“while + contrasting clause”**  
   Use this pattern to compare two different situations.  
   Example: “A smooth surface gives many hair tips a chance to touch, while a very rough surface may leave fewer contact points.”
