# How a Spider Web Stops a Flying Insect

A spider web can look almost invisible, yet it may stop an insect flying at considerable speed. The web does not work like a hard wall. Instead, it combines strong silk, flexible design, sticky surfaces, and careful placement. Each part performs a different job during the few moments between impact and capture.

Spider silk begins as liquid proteins inside special glands in the spider's body. The liquid travels through tiny organs called spinnerets. As it is pulled out, the proteins become solid fibers. A spider can produce several types of silk, each with different properties. Some are strong and relatively stiff, while others stretch much farther before breaking.

In a classic circular orb web, the outer frame and the lines that point toward the center provide support. These radial threads are usually strong and not very sticky. The spider first builds this basic structure. It then adds a spiral line around the web. The capture spiral is more elastic and is often covered with small drops of glue.

When a flying insect hits the web, its motion carries energy. If the web were completely stiff, the silk or the insect might bounce away. Instead, the threads bend and stretch. This movement spreads the force across a wider part of the web and over a slightly longer time. As a result, no single thread has to absorb all the energy at once.

The sticky drops on the capture spiral help prevent the insect from escaping immediately. They can hold onto hairs and other structures on its body. As the insect struggles, it may touch more threads and become further trapped. However, a web must balance stickiness with flexibility. Glue that is too dry may not hold prey, while very wet or damaged silk may perform poorly.

The web also acts as an information network. An impact sends vibrations through the silk. By placing its legs on different threads, the spider can detect where the movement began and learn something about its strength. Wind, falling leaves, and trapped insects create different vibration patterns. The spider uses these clues to decide whether to approach, wait, or hide.

Why does the spider not become trapped in its own web? It often moves mainly along the less sticky frame and radial threads. Its feet make limited contact with the silk, and it places each step carefully. Some physical features of the feet may also reduce sticking. The answer is therefore more complex than the common idea that a spider is simply covered in oil.

Not every spider builds an orb web. Some create flat sheets, funnels, messy three-dimensional networks, or small nets that they throw over prey. Others do not use capture webs at all and hunt by running or jumping. Web design reflects the spider's environment and hunting method.

Many spiders repair damaged sections or eat old silk and reuse some of its materials. A web is therefore not just a passive trap. It is a lightweight structure, a shock absorber, a sticky capture tool, and a communication system—all built from protein fibers made inside a small animal.

## Important Vocabulary

1. **invisible** — impossible or very difficult to see; 看不见的，难以察觉的
2. **silk** — a strong, fine fiber produced by spiders and some insects; 丝
3. **gland** — an organ that produces and releases a substance; 腺体
4. **spinneret** — an organ through which a spider produces silk; 纺器
5. **fiber** — a thin thread-like piece of material; 纤维
6. **radial** — spreading outward from a central point; 放射状的
7. **elastic** — able to stretch and return toward its original shape; 有弹性的
8. **impact** — the forceful contact of one object with another; 撞击，冲击
9. **vibration** — a quick repeated movement backward and forward; 振动
10. **prey** — an animal that is hunted and eaten by another animal; 猎物

## Useful Grammar and Sentence Patterns

1. **“As + clause, + clause”**  
   Use this pattern to show that two events develop together.  
   Example: “As it is pulled out, the proteins become solid fibers.”

2. **“If + past simple, + might + verb”**  
   Use this pattern to describe an imagined situation and its possible result.  
   Example: “If the web were completely stiff, the silk or the insect might bounce away.”

3. **“not just A; it is also B”**  
   Use this pattern to show that something has an additional, less obvious function.  
   Example: “A web is not just a passive trap; it is also a communication system.”
