# Why Screens Make Your Eyes Feel Tired

After several hours on a computer or phone, your eyes may feel dry, heavy, or slightly painful. Words can become difficult to focus on, and you may develop a headache. This group of temporary problems is often called digital eye strain. The screen itself is not the only cause. The way we look at it, the surrounding light, and the position of our bodies all play a part.

One important reason is blinking. People normally blink many times each minute, spreading tears across the surface of the eyes. These tears keep the eyes smooth and comfortable. While reading or watching a screen carefully, however, we often blink less frequently or do not close our eyes completely. As a result, tears evaporate faster and the eyes begin to feel dry or irritated.

Continuous close focus also requires effort. A small group of muscles helps each eye adjust its lens so that nearby objects appear clear. When you look at one distance for a long period, these muscles have little chance to relax. The problem can become worse if the text is too small, the screen is too close, or your glasses are not suitable for the viewing distance.

Light and contrast matter as well. A bright screen in a dark room forces your visual system to deal with a large difference in brightness. Reflections from windows or lamps can create glare, making text harder to see. You may then narrow your eyes, move your head forward, or sit in an uncomfortable position. Eye strain can therefore be connected with pain in the neck, shoulders, or back.

Short, regular breaks can make screen use more comfortable. One popular method is the 20-20-20 rule: every twenty minutes, look at something about twenty feet away for at least twenty seconds. The exact numbers are less important than regularly changing your focus. During a break, blink slowly several times and allow your eyes to rest. Standing up and moving your shoulders can also reduce physical tension.

You can improve your workspace too. Increase the text size instead of leaning closer. Place the screen roughly an arm’s length away and keep its top near or slightly below eye level. Reduce reflections, match the screen brightness to the room, and avoid having a strong light directly behind the screen. If your eyes often feel dry, remember to blink rather than simply turning up the brightness.

Most screen-related tiredness improves after rest, but ongoing pain, strong redness, double vision, or frequent headaches should not be ignored. An eye-care professional can check whether another problem is present. Screens are useful tools, yet our eyes were not designed to stare at one close distance without a break. Small changes in how you work can make a long study session much more comfortable.

## Important Vocabulary

1. **strain** — physical discomfort caused by using part of the body too much; 疲劳，劳损
2. **temporary** — lasting for only a limited time; 暂时的
3. **blink** — to close and open the eyes quickly; 眨眼
4. **evaporate** — to change from a liquid into a gas; 蒸发
5. **irritated** — sore, uncomfortable, or slightly inflamed; 刺痛的，不适的
6. **adjust** — to change something slightly so that it works better; 调整
7. **contrast** — a clear difference between two things; 对比，反差
8. **glare** — strong, unpleasant light that makes seeing difficult; 眩光
9. **tension** — tightness in the body or a feeling of pressure; 紧张，绷紧
10. **ongoing** — continuing to exist or develop; 持续的

## Useful Grammar and Sentence Patterns

1. **“The way + clause …”**  
   Use this structure to talk about how an action is performed.  
   Example: “The way we look at it, the surrounding light, and the position of our bodies all play a part.”

2. **“instead of + verb-ing”**  
   Use this pattern to suggest one action as a better choice than another.  
   Example: “Increase the text size instead of leaning closer.”

3. **“whether + alternative or possibility”**  
   Use **whether** when something needs to be checked or decided.  
   Example: “An eye-care professional can check whether another problem is present.”
