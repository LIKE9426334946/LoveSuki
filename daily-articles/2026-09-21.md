# How Seeds Know Which Way to Grow

A seed may begin growing underground where there is no light. Even so, its first root usually grows downward, while its young shoot moves upward toward the surface. The seed does not have eyes or a map. Instead, cells inside the plant sense gravity, light, moisture, and other signals from the environment.

The response to gravity is called gravitropism. Roots normally show positive gravitropism because they grow in the direction of gravity. Shoots show negative gravitropism because they grow in the opposite direction. This behavior helps roots enter the soil, where they can collect water and minerals, while leaves move toward the air and sunlight.

Near the tip of a young root is a small protective structure called the root cap. Some cells inside it contain dense, starch-filled parts known as statoliths. Because they are heavier than the material around them, statoliths settle toward the lowest side of each cell. Their position provides information about which way is down.

The plant turns this information into uneven growth. A chemical messenger called auxin is moved toward one side of the root. In roots, a high level of auxin slows the lengthening of cells. The cells on the upper side therefore grow longer than those on the lower side, causing the root to bend downward. If the seed is turned, the statoliths settle into a new position, and the root gradually changes direction.

Auxin also helps shoots respond to gravity, but its effect there is different. In a young shoot, more auxin generally encourages cells to lengthen. When auxin gathers on the lower side, those cells grow faster, bending the shoot upward. The same chemical can therefore produce different results depending on the kind of plant tissue.

Once the shoot reaches light, another response becomes important: phototropism. Light-sensitive proteins near the shoot tip detect where light is coming from. Auxin then becomes more concentrated on the shaded side. Cells on that side lengthen more, so the shoot bends toward the light. This position helps the young leaves collect the energy needed for photosynthesis.

Gravity and light are not the only guides. Roots can also respond to water, touch, and certain chemicals in the soil. When signals compete, the plant combines them. For example, a root may grow around a stone while still moving mostly downward. Its final path reflects several responses working together rather than a single command.

You can observe these abilities with a simple experiment. Place a sprouting bean in a clear container, wait until its root has grown, and then turn the container on its side. Over the next day or two, the root should curve downward again, while the shoot bends upward. Keep the seed moist and avoid damaging the delicate tips.

A seed may appear still and simple, but it is constantly measuring its surroundings. By changing how quickly cells grow on different sides, it can find a useful direction without seeing where it is going.

## Important Vocabulary

1. **seed** — the part of a plant that can develop into a new plant; 种子
2. **shoot** — a young stem or new growth above the ground; 幼芽，新枝
3. **gravity** — the force that pulls objects toward the Earth; 重力
4. **gravitropism** — plant growth in response to gravity; 向重力性
5. **root cap** — the protective structure covering the tip of a root; 根冠
6. **settle** — to move downward and rest in a lower position; 沉降，落定
7. **auxin** — a plant hormone that controls growth and other responses; 生长素
8. **tissue** — a group of similar cells that perform a particular function; 组织
9. **phototropism** — plant growth in response to light; 向光性
10. **delicate** — easily damaged and needing careful treatment; 脆弱的，需要小心处理的

## Useful Grammar and Sentence Patterns

1. **“Even so, + clause”**  
   Use this pattern to introduce a surprising result despite the previous fact.  
   Example: “A seed may begin growing where there is no light. Even so, its first root usually grows downward.”

2. **“causing + noun + to + verb”**  
   Use this pattern to explain the result of an action or situation.  
   Example: “The cells on the upper side grow longer, causing the root to bend downward.”

3. **“depending on + noun”**  
   Use this pattern when a result changes according to a condition.  
   Example: “The same chemical can produce different results depending on the kind of plant tissue.”
