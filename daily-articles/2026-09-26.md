# Why Cutting Onions Makes You Cry

Cutting an onion can turn a simple cooking task into a surprisingly emotional experience. Your eyes begin to sting, tears appear, and you may need to step away from the cutting board. The onion is not trying to defend itself in a conscious way, but its cells contain chemicals that create an irritating gas when the tissue is damaged.

An onion is made of many tightly packed cells. Inside those cells, certain sulfur-containing substances and enzymes are stored in separate areas. As long as the onion remains whole, they do not mix very much. When you cut, crush, or chop the onion, the cell walls break. The separated chemicals then come into contact and begin a series of reactions.

One important product of these reactions is a volatile sulfur compound. “Volatile” means that a substance can easily become a gas and move through the air. This gas rises from the cutting board and can reach your eyes. There, it mixes with the thin layer of water that normally covers the surface of the eye and creates irritating chemicals.

Your eyes are very sensitive because their surface needs protection from dust, smoke, and harmful substances. Nerves in the eye detect the irritation and quickly send a warning signal to the brain. In response, the tear glands produce extra liquid. The purpose of these tears is practical: they help wash the irritating material away.

This explains why onion tears are different from emotional tears. In both cases, liquid comes from the tear glands, but the trigger is different. Onion tears are a protective reflex caused by chemical irritation.

Several kitchen methods can reduce the problem. A sharp knife helps because it damages fewer cells than a dull knife, so fewer irritating chemicals are released. Good ventilation can also carry the gas away from your face. Cutting near a kitchen fan or an open window may therefore help.

Cooling the onion before cutting can slow chemical reactions and reduce how quickly the irritating compounds enter the air. Some people also cut onions under running water or near a bowl of water. Water may absorb part of the gas, although these methods can be inconvenient and are not always equally effective.

Your cutting position matters too. Keeping your face farther from the onion gives the gas more space to spread before it reaches your eyes. Avoid rubbing your eyes while cooking, especially if onion juice is on your fingers, because direct contact can cause much stronger irritation.

Different onions may produce different amounts of the irritating compound. Their variety, age, growing conditions, and storage can all affect their chemistry. This is why one onion may make you cry heavily while another causes almost no reaction.

The next time an onion makes your eyes water, remember that the tears are not a sign that your eyes are weak. They are part of a fast protective system. A damaged onion releases chemicals, your eyes detect them, and your body immediately begins washing them away.

## Important Vocabulary

1. **sting** — to cause a sharp, burning, or painful feeling; 刺痛
2. **tissue** — a group of similar cells that form part of a living thing; 组织
3. **enzyme** — a protein that helps a chemical reaction happen faster; 酶
4. **reaction** — a chemical process in which substances change into new substances; 化学反应
5. **volatile** — able to become a gas easily; 易挥发的
6. **irritating** — causing discomfort, pain, or inflammation; 刺激性的
7. **surface** — the outer or top layer of something; 表面
8. **reflex** — an automatic action of the body in response to a stimulus; 反射
9. **ventilation** — the movement of fresh air through a space; 通风
10. **compound** — a substance formed from two or more chemical elements; 化合物

## Useful Grammar and Sentence Patterns

1. **“As long as + clause, + result”**  
   Use this pattern to say that something remains true while a condition continues.  
   Example: “As long as the onion remains whole, the chemicals do not mix very much.”

2. **“In response, + subject + verb”**  
   Use this pattern to describe an action caused by something that happened before.  
   Example: “In response, the tear glands produce extra liquid.”

3. **“This is why + clause”**  
   Use this pattern to connect an explanation with an observed result.  
   Example: “This is why one onion may make you cry heavily while another causes almost no reaction.”
