# How Trees Lift Water Without a Pump

A tall tree may carry water from the soil to leaves more than fifty meters above the ground. Yet a tree has no heart, no mechanical pump, and no moving tubes. How can water travel so far upward against gravity? The answer depends on sunlight, tiny openings in leaves, and a special property of water.

Water first enters a tree through its roots. Near the ends of young roots are many tiny root hairs. They greatly increase the area that touches the soil, allowing the tree to absorb more water and dissolved minerals. From the roots, water enters long, narrow tubes called xylem. These tubes run through the roots, trunk, branches, and leaves.

The main force that moves water begins in the leaves. Leaves have small openings called stomata, which allow gases to enter and leave. When the stomata are open, some water changes into vapor and escapes into the air. This process is called transpiration. As water leaves a leaf, it creates a slight pulling force on the water behind it. It is similar to drinking through a straw, although a tree pulls water by evaporation rather than with a mouth.

This system works because water molecules attract one another. This attraction is known as cohesion. Inside the narrow xylem tubes, the molecules form a continuous column of water from the roots toward the leaves. When molecules at the top move out of a leaf, they pull the rest of the column upward. Water also sticks slightly to the walls of the xylem. This property, called adhesion, helps the column resist gravity.

Sunlight provides the energy for evaporation, so water usually moves faster on bright, warm days. Wind can also increase water loss by carrying moist air away from leaf surfaces. However, losing too much water is dangerous. During hot or dry conditions, a tree can partly close its stomata. This reduces water loss, but it also limits the carbon dioxide needed for photosynthesis. The tree must balance saving water with making food.

The movement of water does more than prevent leaves from drying out. It carries minerals from the soil, cools the plant, and supplies water for photosynthesis. Still, the system has limits. If the soil becomes extremely dry or air enters the xylem, the continuous water column may break. Then some branches can no longer receive enough water.

The next time you stand under a large tree, imagine millions of water molecules moving silently through its trunk. No motor is pushing them. Instead, the tree uses the energy of sunlight and the natural behavior of water to operate a remarkably effective transport system.

## Important Vocabulary

1. **gravity** — the force that pulls objects toward the Earth; 重力
2. **absorb** — to take in a liquid, gas, or other substance; 吸收
3. **dissolved** — mixed into a liquid so that it becomes part of the liquid; 溶解的
4. **xylem** — plant tissue that carries water and minerals upward; 木质部
5. **vapor** — very small drops or the gas form of a liquid in the air; 水汽，蒸气
6. **transpiration** — the process by which a plant loses water through its leaves; 蒸腾作用
7. **evaporation** — the process in which a liquid changes into a gas; 蒸发
8. **cohesion** — the attraction between molecules of the same substance; 内聚力
9. **adhesion** — the attraction between different substances or surfaces; 附着力
10. **continuous** — continuing without a break; 连续的

## Useful Grammar and Sentence Patterns

1. **“allow + noun + to + verb”**  
   Use this pattern to explain that something makes an action possible.  
   Example: “Leaves have small openings called stomata, which allow gases to enter and leave.”

2. **“As + event, + result”**  
   Use this pattern when one change happens at the same time as another.  
   Example: “As water leaves a leaf, it creates a slight pulling force on the water behind it.”

3. **“The next time + clause, imagine …”**  
   Use this pattern to invite someone to think differently during a future experience.  
   Example: “The next time you stand under a large tree, imagine millions of water molecules moving silently through its trunk.”
