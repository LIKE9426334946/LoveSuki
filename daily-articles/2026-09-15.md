# How a QR Code Survives Damage

QR codes appear on menus, tickets, packages, and payment screens. You point a phone at a square pattern, and a website or message opens almost immediately. What is more surprising is that a QR code may still work when part of it is dirty, scratched, or covered. This ability comes from careful design and a method called error correction.

A QR code stores information in a grid of black and white squares called modules. The pattern can represent numbers, letters, or other data. Before the data is placed into the grid, it is changed into a long series of binary values—zeros and ones. Groups of these values are then arranged across the code according to fixed rules.

The three large squares near the corners are called finder patterns. They help a camera locate the code and understand its direction. Smaller patterns help the reader judge size and correct the image when the code is viewed at an angle. A quiet white border around the outside also separates the QR code from nearby text or pictures.

However, the code does not contain only the original message. When it is created, extra information is calculated and added. This repeated or supporting data allows the reader to rebuild some missing parts. The system commonly uses a mathematical technique known as Reed–Solomon error correction. You do not need to recover every damaged square correctly. Instead, the extra data provides enough clues to reconstruct the message.

QR codes can be created with different levels of error correction. A low level leaves more space for the original message but repairs less damage. A high level uses more of the grid for supporting data, so it can often survive greater damage. Depending on the chosen level, a code may recover when roughly 7 to 30 percent of its data is unreadable. This does not mean that any 30 percent can safely disappear. Damage to important location patterns or a badly placed mark may still prevent scanning.

Lighting and camera position matter too. Glare can hide parts of a shiny code, while shadows may reduce the difference between black and white areas. Moving the phone slightly, cleaning the surface, or increasing the distance can help the camera see the full pattern. Printing a QR code too small can also make its modules difficult to separate.

Error correction is useful, but it does not make a QR code trustworthy. A damaged code may still lead to the correct address, yet a perfectly clear code can lead to a harmful website. Before opening a link, check the address shown by your phone, especially when the code is on a public sign or asks for payment or personal information.

The next time a scratched QR code scans successfully, remember that your phone is doing more than reading visible squares. It is using structure, repeated information, and mathematics to solve a small puzzle in a fraction of a second.

## Important Vocabulary

1. **scratch** — a thin mark or cut on a surface; 划痕，刮痕
2. **error correction** — a method of finding and repairing mistakes in stored or transmitted data; 纠错
3. **grid** — a pattern of crossing lines that forms squares; 网格
4. **module** — one small black or white square in a QR code; 模块，单元
5. **binary** — using only two values, usually zero and one; 二进制的
6. **locate** — to find the exact position of something; 定位，找到
7. **reconstruct** — to build or form something again from available information; 重建，还原
8. **unreadable** — impossible or very difficult to read; 无法读取的
9. **glare** — strong reflected light that makes seeing difficult; 眩光
10. **trustworthy** — able to be trusted as honest or safe; 值得信赖的

## Useful Grammar and Sentence Patterns

1. **“What is more surprising is that …”**  
   Use this pattern to introduce an additional fact that is unexpected.  
   Example: “What is more surprising is that a QR code may still work when part of it is covered.”

2. **“Depending on + noun, + result”**  
   Use this pattern when a result changes according to a condition.  
   Example: “Depending on the chosen level, a code may recover when roughly 7 to 30 percent of its data is unreadable.”

3. **“does not mean that …”**  
   Use this pattern to prevent an explanation from being understood too broadly.  
   Example: “This does not mean that any 30 percent can safely disappear.”
