# How Soap Removes Grease

If you wash an oily plate with plain water, much of the grease remains. The water may move across the plate, but it does not carry the oil away. Add a little soap, however, and the grease becomes much easier to remove. This familiar result depends on the unusual structure of soap molecules.

Water molecules are attracted to other water molecules. Oil molecules also prefer to stay with oil. Because water and oil have different chemical properties, they do not mix well. That is why oil forms separate drops or a thin layer on the surface of water. Hot water can soften grease and make it move more easily, but heat alone does not solve the mixing problem.

A soap molecule has two very different ends. One end is attracted to water and is described as hydrophilic, meaning “water-loving.” The other end avoids water but is attracted to oils and fats. It is called hydrophobic, or “water-fearing.” This two-part structure allows soap to connect substances that normally stay apart.

When soap meets a greasy surface, its hydrophobic ends enter the grease. The hydrophilic ends remain in the surrounding water. As you rub the surface, the grease breaks into smaller pieces. Soap molecules gather around each piece, with their oil-loving ends pointing inward and their water-loving ends pointing outward. They form tiny round structures called micelles.

The outside of a micelle interacts easily with water, while the grease is trapped inside. As a result, the water can now carry the grease away when you rinse the plate or your hands. Rubbing helps because it loosens dirt and increases contact between the soap, water, and oily material. Flowing water then removes the micelles from the surface.

Soap also helps water spread. Water naturally forms rounded drops because of surface tension, which is caused by attraction between its molecules. Soap reduces this tension, so water can cover a larger area and enter small spaces more effectively. This makes cleaning easier on dishes, clothes, and skin.

Using more soap does not always mean better cleaning. Once there is enough soap to surround the available grease, adding a large extra amount may simply waste the product and require more water for rinsing. The correct amount depends on how dirty the object is, how much water you use, and the type of cleaner.

Soap may look simple, but each drop contains countless molecules working in an organized way. One end holds oil, the other stays with water, and together they turn a difficult cleaning problem into something that can be washed down the drain.

## Important Vocabulary

1. **grease** — thick oil or fat, especially when it makes something dirty; 油脂
2. **attracted** — drawn toward something by a force or interest; 被吸引的
3. **property** — a quality or characteristic of a substance or object; 性质，特性
4. **structure** — the way the parts of something are arranged; 结构
5. **hydrophilic** — attracted to water or able to mix with it; 亲水的
6. **hydrophobic** — avoiding water or not mixing easily with it; 疏水的
7. **surrounding** — existing or happening around something; 周围的
8. **micelle** — a tiny group of molecules that can trap oil in water; 胶束
9. **rinse** — to wash something with clean water; 冲洗
10. **tension** — a force that makes a surface behave like a stretched layer; 张力

## Useful Grammar and Sentence Patterns

1. **“That is why + clause”**  
   Use this pattern to introduce a result explained by the previous sentence.  
   Example: “That is why oil forms separate drops or a thin layer on the surface of water.”

2. **“with + noun + verb-ing”**  
   Use this pattern to describe how parts are positioned or what they are doing.  
   Example: “Soap molecules gather around each piece, with their oil-loving ends pointing inward.”

3. **“Once there is enough …, + result”**  
   Use this pattern to explain what happens after a sufficient amount or condition is reached.  
   Example: “Once there is enough soap to surround the available grease, adding more may simply waste the product.”
