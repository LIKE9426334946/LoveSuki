# Why Wet Clothes Dry Without Boiling

Water boils at about 100 degrees Celsius under normal air pressure, yet wet clothes can dry on a cool day. A puddle can also disappear without ever becoming hot. This happens because boiling is not the only way liquid water can enter the air. Water can also change into gas through evaporation.

In liquid water, molecules are always moving. They do not all move at the same speed. Some travel slowly, while others briefly carry more energy. At the surface of a wet shirt, a faster molecule may have enough energy to escape the attraction of nearby molecules. It then enters the air as invisible water vapor. Because this process happens at the surface, evaporation can occur at almost any ordinary temperature.

Boiling is different. When water boils, bubbles of water vapor form throughout the liquid, not only at the surface. This requires the vapor pressure inside the bubbles to become strong enough to resist the pressure around them. At sea level, that usually happens near 100 degrees Celsius. Evaporation is slower, but it does not require the whole body of water to reach its boiling point.

Temperature still matters. In warm water, molecules move faster on average, so more of them can escape each second. Warm air can also support a larger amount of water vapor before becoming saturated. Therefore, clothes generally dry faster in warm conditions than in cold ones.

Humidity is equally important. Humidity describes how much water vapor is already in the air. When the surrounding air is dry, water molecules can leave the fabric easily, and relatively few return to it. When the air is very humid, many water molecules are already present. More of them move back toward the wet surface, so the net loss of water becomes slower.

This explains why laundry may dry slowly on a warm but humid day. It also explains why clothes can dry in cold winter air if that air is dry. Temperature affects molecular speed, while humidity affects how much additional vapor the air can accept.

Moving air speeds up drying too. As water evaporates, a thin layer of humid air develops close to the fabric. If the air remains still, this layer reduces further evaporation. Wind or a fan carries the humid air away and replaces it with drier air. This allows more water molecules to escape.

The shape and material of the clothes also affect drying time. Spreading a shirt out exposes more wet surface to the air. Folding it creates hidden areas where humid air can remain trapped. Thick fabrics hold water in many small spaces and give it a longer path to the surface. Synthetic sports clothing often dries faster than thick cotton because it usually absorbs less water and is designed to move moisture outward.

Evaporation also cools the remaining liquid and the material around it. The fastest molecules are the ones most likely to escape, so the molecules left behind have lower average energy. This is why wet skin feels cool, especially in moving air, and why sweating helps the body release heat.

To dry laundry efficiently, increase airflow, spread each item widely, and reduce indoor humidity if possible. Heat can help, but fresh moving air may be just as important. Wet clothes do not need to boil; their water simply escapes one energetic molecule at a time.

## Important Vocabulary

1. **evaporation** — the process in which a liquid changes into gas at its surface; 蒸发
2. **molecule** — the smallest unit of a substance that keeps its chemical properties; 分子
3. **vapor** — a substance in its gas form, especially one usually found as a liquid; 蒸气
4. **attraction** — a force that pulls things toward one another; 吸引力
5. **pressure** — force applied to a particular area; 压力
6. **saturated** — unable to hold or contain any more of something; 饱和的
7. **humidity** — the amount of water vapor in the air; 湿度
8. **surrounding** — existing or located around something; 周围的
9. **fabric** — material made by weaving or joining fibers, especially for clothing; 织物，布料
10. **absorb** — to take in a liquid, gas, or other substance; 吸收

## Useful Grammar and Sentence Patterns

1. **“This happens because + clause”**  
   Use this pattern to introduce the reason for an observed result.  
   Example: “This happens because boiling is not the only way liquid water can enter the air.”

2. **“The + comparative, the + comparative”**  
   Use this pattern to show that two conditions change together.  
   Example: “The drier the air is, the faster wet clothes can usually dry.”

3. **“This is why + clause”**  
   Use this pattern to connect a scientific explanation with an everyday effect.  
   Example: “This is why wet skin feels cool, especially in moving air.”
